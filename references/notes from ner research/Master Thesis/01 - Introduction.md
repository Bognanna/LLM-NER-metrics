## Motivation

The rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs) has significantly influenced the development of other fields related to natural language processing (NLP) and language technologies [https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2409.19508] . There are many NLP tasks that can be addressed using LLMs. [https://doi.org/10.1007/s11704-025-50472-3]. One such task is named entity recognition (NER). Before the rise of LLMs, approaches to NER were primarily based on handcraft rules, linguistic patterns, gazetteers, machine learning techniques [https://nlp.cs.nyu.edu/sekine/papers/li07.pdf] and subsequently deep learning and embeddings [https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1812.09449]. These approaches produce structured outputs that can be evaluated using metrics focused on whether the predicted entity boundaries and entity types exactly match the expected annotation. [https://nlp.cs.nyu.edu/sekine/papers/li07.pdf] However, with the rise of LLMs, new approaches to the NER task have emerged. Instead of predicting spans directly, LLMs can be prompted to generate a list of entities based on the input text. Because these entities are generated rather than extracted verbatim, they may be paraphrased, reordered, or expressed in a different grammatical form compared to their original occurrence in the text. [https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2305.14450] This creates a challenge in evaluating the performance of such models. Applying traditional exact-match metrics to outputs generated by LLMs may incorrectly classify semantically correct answers as incorrect simply because they differ lexically or grammatically from the expected output. Because of this problem, several approaches to evaluating LLM-generated responses have emerged - in this thesis I mention three of them. The first one is to adapting existing metrics to became more flexible. For example by adapting soft-matching strategy to F1-score metric. Methods based on this approach  are indeed more adjusted to evalute LLM responses, because they are more robust for example for adding an extra word to the output span, however still they are not capable to capture paraphrases. The second one approach is making an evaluation by another LLM as a judge. Evaluation methods based on this approach can capture semantic niuances nearly the same as the human as a judge, however these methods are highly variable on the prompt and criteria used to instruct the evaluation model and it is costfull, because evaluating each instance is calling again a prompt. The third approach is based on embedding vectors. Methods based on embedding vectors can also capture paraphrases, but not so well as LLMs, however their are not so costfull and can apply to many cases without fine tuning prompt each time as it is in case of LLMs. Because I am resorcefull concerned I decide to experiment with the third approach and developed a new metric based on embeddings to evaluate LLMs performance in NER task.

The goal of this thesis is to present a new metric CDEF that based on enitites embeddings measure the quality of LLM responses for NER task. In the first part of the thesis I present the related work, starting from the information extraction task, then going more specifict to named entity recognition and presenting how LLMs are used in NER task. In the second part I present results of my work: a new metric CDEF, the theoretical bachground standing behind it, example of its usefullned on use cases and its performance on the benchmark tests.
